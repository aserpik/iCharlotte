MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES IN REPLY

I.
INTRODUCTION

Plaintiff's Opposition to Attorney Defendants' Motion for Attorneys' Fees fails at every turn. Rather than presenting credible evidence or authority to rebut Attorney Defendants' well-documented fee request, Plaintiff offers only unsupported assertions, inapplicable case law, and the self-serving — and legally irrelevant — declaration of her own counsel regarding her personal billing rates. The Opposition does not and cannot overcome the mandatory nature of fee awards under Code of Civil Procedure Section 425.16(c), the well-documented billing records submitted by Attorney Defendants, or the extensive authority establishing the reasonableness of the rates and hours at issue.

Attorney Defendants prevailed on the Anti-SLAPP Motion in the trial court. The Court of Appeal affirmed. Fees are mandatory. The only question is the amount. Plaintiff's proposed reduction to $21,000 — roughly one-third of the fees actually incurred — is arbitrary, unsupported by any credible evidence, and contrary to established law. As set forth below, Attorney Defendants respectfully request that the Court award the full $66,060 in attorneys' fees.

II.
PLAINTIFF'S EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS ARE MERITLESS AND SHOULD BE OVERRULED

As a threshold matter, Plaintiff's evidentiary objections to the Serpik Declaration should be overruled in their entirety.

A. Mr. Serpik Has Personal Knowledge of Mr. Swain's Work and Rates

Plaintiff objects to paragraphs 20 and 22 of the Serpik Declaration — Mr. Serpik's testimony regarding Mr. Swain's experience, billing rate, and hours — on grounds of lack of personal knowledge, hearsay, lack of relevance, and lack of foundation. Each objection is meritless.

Mr. Serpik was the supervising attorney on the appeal. He directly oversaw Mr. Swain's work, reviewed the billing entries in his firm's billing system, and has personal knowledge of the work Mr. Swain performed. A supervising attorney's testimony about the hours worked by attorneys in his firm is the standard and accepted method of establishing fees. Korchemny v. Piterman (2021) 68 Cal.App.5th 1032, 1049 (rejecting identical objections and holding that a single attorney's declaration as to work performed by others in the firm is sufficient); Los Angeles Unified School Dist. v. Torres Construction Corp. (2020) 57 Cal.App.5th 480, 516 (affirming fee award based on single attorney's declaration for all attorneys at the firm); Syers Properties III, Inc. v. Rankin (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 691, 695 (accepting attorney's declaration as to work performed by his associate and paralegals); Minser v. Collect Access, LLC (2023) 92 Cal.App.5th 781, 797 (a single attorney with knowledge of his firm's billing rates may properly provide a declaration as to how much each attorney worked).

Mr. Serpik's testimony about Mr. Swain's hours and rates is not hearsay. It is based on his personal review of firm billing records maintained in the ordinary course of business and his direct supervision of the work. Even if the billing records themselves were considered, they would be admissible under the business records exception. (Evid. Code, § 1271.) Moreover, the Court is "not bound by technical rules of evidence" in fixing attorneys' fees and "is merely seeking information upon which to base its order." Frank v. Frank (1963) 213 Cal.App.2d 135, 138.

The relevance objection is frivolous. Mr. Swain's hours and rate are directly at issue in this motion.

B. No Declaration from Mr. Swain Is Required

Plaintiff argues that Mr. Swain's hours are "inadmissible" because Mr. Swain did not provide his own declaration. This is wrong as a matter of law. There is no requirement that each timekeeper submit an individual declaration. As the authorities cited above make clear, a single attorney's declaration is sufficient to establish the hours worked by all attorneys at the firm. Raining Data Corp. v. Barrenechea (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1363, 1375 ("an award of attorney fees may be based on counsel's declarations, without production of detailed time records"). Indeed, time records are not even required — an estimate of hours suffices so long as it is reasonable. Id.; Margolin v. Regional Planning Com. (1982) 134 Cal.App.3d 999, 1007.

III.
THE HOURS EXPENDED ARE REASONABLE AND WELL-DOCUMENTED

A. The Hours on the Anti-SLAPP Motion Are Reasonable

Plaintiff contends that the 36.9 hours spent on the Anti-SLAPP motion should be cut in half. This argument is based on the false premise that work by Mr. Serpik and Ms. Aleagha was "duplicative." It was not.

As stated in Attorney Defendants' moving papers, Mr. Serpik spent 28.9 hours on the Anti-SLAPP motion itself — including reviewing the complaint, investigating the underlying litigation, researching the law, preparing the motion and reply briefs, communicating with clients, and arguing the motion. (Serpik Decl., ¶¶ 3, 8-9 & Exh. 5.) Ms. Aleagha separately spent 8 hours preparing the original Motion for Fees — a different document entirely from the Anti-SLAPP motion. (Serpik Decl., ¶ 14 & Exh. 6.) These were two different tasks performed at two different times for two different purposes. There is nothing duplicative about this allocation.

Plaintiff's suggestion that Mr. Serpik billed 28.9 hours "to also draft the motion" that Ms. Aleagha drafted fundamentally mischaracterizes the record. Mr. Serpik's 28.9 hours were spent on the Anti-SLAPP motion itself, not on the fees motion. Ms. Aleagha's 8 hours were spent on the subsequent fees motion. Plaintiff either did not read the papers or is deliberately conflating the two.

Moreover, the scope of the work required was driven entirely by Plaintiff's own litigation conduct. Plaintiff's complaint contained six causes of action asserting disparate theories of liability. Her opposition to the Anti-SLAPP motion included a 15-page memorandum, 183 pages of exhibits, and 20 evidentiary objections. (Mtn. at 7:10-12.) The Court itself postponed the hearing due to the sheer volume of motions necessitated by Plaintiff's claims. (Id. at 7:12-14.) Under these circumstances, 28.9 hours for the Anti-SLAPP motion — encompassing the full lifecycle from investigation through post-hearing matters — is eminently reasonable. See, e.g., Marshall v. Webster (2020) 54 Cal.App.5th 275, 277 (affirming $79,000 award); Lunada Biomedical v. Nunez (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 459, 488 (affirming awards of $104,293.75 and $57,765.63).

B. The Hours on the Appeal Are Reasonable

Plaintiff contends that the 75.2 combined hours on the appeal should be cut in half, arguing that the work between Mr. Serpik and Mr. Swain was "duplicative." This argument ignores the reality of appellate practice and the record in this case.

1. Two Attorneys on an Appeal Is Standard Practice

It is routine and expected practice for more than one attorney to work on an appeal. Appellate litigation involves extensive research, drafting, editing, and record review. The notion that only one attorney should handle an appeal — particularly one involving complex Anti-SLAPP issues that went through full briefing — is contrary to the reality of modern legal practice. Plaintiff cites no authority for the proposition that the use of two attorneys on an appeal is per se unreasonable or duplicative.

2. The Work Was Not Duplicative

Plaintiff complains that Mr. Swain performed "tasks that Mr. Serpik had already done" such as reviewing the file and case law. This reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of appellate practice. When a new attorney is brought onto an appeal — even where trial counsel continues to participate — the appellate attorney must independently review the record and relevant authority. This is not "duplicative" work; it is essential preparation that any competent attorney must perform before drafting appellate briefs. A lawyer cannot competently draft a brief based on someone else's understanding of the record.

3. The Time Entries Are Sufficiently Detailed

Plaintiff cherry-picks Mr. Swain's 9.9-hour entry for editing the Respondent's Appellate Brief, suggesting this is excessive. But editing a full appellate brief — which must be comprehensive, meticulously cited, and comply with all appellate rules — is a time-intensive task. The Respondent's Brief addressed multiple issues raised by Plaintiff on appeal. (See Serpik Decl., Exhs. 10-14.) Attorney Defendants also filed a Motion to Dismiss, an Opposition to Plaintiff's Motion to File Under Seal, and an Opposition to Plaintiff's Motion to Augment — all of which required additional time. (Serpik Decl., ¶¶ 28-30 & Exhs. 12-14.) Plaintiff's suggestion that 9.9 hours of editing on a brief that ultimately prevailed should be cut in half is arbitrary and unsupported.

4. Mr. Swain's Practice Area Is Irrelevant

Plaintiff's repeated characterization of Mr. Swain as a "probate attorney" who has "no experience in civil litigation" is misleading and irrelevant. First, Mr. Swain was employed by Bordin Semmer LLP during the relevant period, where he "dedicated his time ... primarily to law and motion and appellate matters." (Serpik Decl., ¶ 20.) The fact that Mr. Swain later joined a firm where he practices probate litigation does not retroactively negate the work he performed at Bordin Semmer LLP on civil litigation and appellate matters. Second, the Anti-SLAPP statute and the litigation privilege are not specialized "civil litigation" concepts beyond the grasp of any competent attorney — they are standard procedural tools. Third, even if Mr. Swain's current practice focus were relevant, Plaintiff's own exhibit shows that he has experience in "probate, estate, and trust litigation" and "related civil disputes" — he is a litigator, not a transactional attorney. (Dib Decl., Exh. 1.)

Most importantly, the quality of Mr. Swain's work speaks for itself: the Court of Appeal affirmed. Plaintiff cannot credibly argue that Mr. Swain's work was somehow deficient or unnecessary when it contributed to a successful result.

5. 75.2 Hours for a Fully-Briefed Appeal Is Reasonable

The 75.2 total hours for the appeal encompassed: review of the record; research; drafting the Respondent's Brief; a Motion to Dismiss; an Opposition to Plaintiff's Motion to File Under Seal; an Opposition to Plaintiff's Motion to Augment; and all related communications and procedural matters. Plaintiff herself drove the volume of work by filing multiple motions in the Court of Appeal and raising numerous issues on appeal. Under these circumstances, 75.2 hours is well within the range of reasonable time for appellate work. See Wanland v. Law Offices of Mastagni, Holstedt & Chiurazzi (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 15, 21 (fees for appeal of Anti-SLAPP motion are fully recoverable).

IV.
THE BILLING RATES ARE REASONABLE AND WELL-SUPPORTED

A. Plaintiff's Counsel's Personal Billing Rate Is Irrelevant

The centerpiece of Plaintiff's rate challenge is the Declaration of Monica D. Dib, who testifies that her own hourly rate in 2023 was $350 and is currently $450. From this, Plaintiff reasons that Mr. Serpik's rate of $600 and Mr. Swain's rate of $600 must be unreasonable. This argument is a non sequitur.

The relevant inquiry is the "prevailing rate in the community for similar work." PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095. One opposing counsel's self-reported rate is not a market survey. Ms. Dib's personal billing rate reflects her own practice, her own overhead, her own client base, and her own market positioning. It says nothing about the prevailing market rate in Southern California for attorneys with Mr. Serpik's or Mr. Swain's experience handling complex Anti-SLAPP litigation and appeals.

Notably, Ms. Dib does not claim to have any expertise in the Anti-SLAPP statute or appellate practice. She does not identify any fee surveys, market data, or comparable awards. She does not opine on prevailing rates in the community — she merely states her own rate and asserts that Defendants' rates "should be" lower. This is not evidence of market rates; it is advocacy masquerading as a declaration.

B. Attorney Defendants' Rates Are Supported by Objective Evidence

Unlike Plaintiff, Attorney Defendants submitted objective evidence of market rates. The motion is supported by reference to:

(1) The Laffey Matrix, which establishes that the 2021 D.C. rate for attorneys with Mr. Serpik's experience was $452/hour — before adjusting for California cost-of-living or inflation. (Serpik Decl., Exh. 7.)

(2) The Fitzpatrick Matrix, which places the 2021 rate for similarly experienced attorneys at $545/hour in the D.C. market — again, before California and inflation adjustments. (Serpik Decl., Exh. 8.)

(3) The CPI Inflation Calculator, which demonstrates that $545 in 2021 dollars equates to $611.66 in 2023 dollars, and inflation has only increased since. (Mtn. at 8:13-17.)

(4) Case law establishing that rates of $750/hour were "common" in the Los Angeles market as of 2016. Chambers v. Whirlpool Corp. (C.D. Cal. 2016) 214 F.Supp.3d 877, 899.

Courts have routinely relied on the Laffey and Fitzpatrick Matrices as benchmarks for reasonable hourly rates. Syers Properties III, Inc. v. Rankin (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 691, 696; Tukes v. Richard (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 1, 17. Courts have also noted that the Laffey Matrix rates are, if anything, too low for the Los Angeles market. Fernandez v. Victoria Secret Stores, LLC (C.D. Cal. 2008) 2008 WL 8150856, at *14.

Against this well-supported evidentiary showing, Plaintiff offers only Ms. Dib's personal rate. This is insufficient to overcome Attorney Defendants' burden.

C. $600/Hour for Mr. Serpik Is Reasonable

Mr. Serpik has been practicing since 2014 — over 11 years as of 2025. He is a partner at a well-established firm, has extensive complex litigation experience, and his rate of $600/hour is consistent with rates awarded by courts for his work. (Serpik Decl., ¶ 11.) As demonstrated above, objective market data confirms that $600 is well within — and actually below — the prevailing range for attorneys with his experience in Southern California.

Plaintiff's proposed rate of $300/hour for the Anti-SLAPP motion and $350/hour for the appeal is not supported by any authority or evidence. Plaintiff cites Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1324 for the proposition that "courts approve hourly rates as low as $300." But the fact that some courts have approved rates as low as $300 does not establish $300 as the appropriate rate here. That case involved different counsel, different work, and different market conditions nearly two decades ago. The same logic would allow one to argue that because some courts have approved rates of $750, that rate should apply here. The relevant inquiry is the prevailing market rate for the specific attorney and the specific work — and the evidence supports $600/hour.

D. $600/Hour for Mr. Swain Is Reasonable

Mr. Swain was admitted to the California Bar in 2018 and had approximately 6-7 years of experience during the appeal. He dedicated his time at Bordin Semmer LLP primarily to law and motion and appellate matters. (Serpik Decl., ¶ 20.) His rate of $600/hour is consistent with rates for similarly situated attorneys at the firm and in the market.

Plaintiff's suggestion that Mr. Swain should be billed at $300/hour because he is a "probate attorney" fundamentally misunderstands the lodestar analysis. The relevant factors are "the skill and experience of the attorneys, the nature of the work performed, the relevant area of expertise, their customary billing rates, and the prevailing rate charged by attorneys of similar skill and experience." Flannery v. California Highway Patrol (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 629, 632-633. There is no rule that an attorney's rate is tethered to whatever practice area they happen to pursue at a subsequent employer. Mr. Swain was performing civil appellate work at Bordin Semmer LLP, and his rate should reflect the market rate for that work.

E. $450/Hour for Ms. Aleagha Is Reasonable

Plaintiff proposes that Ms. Aleagha's rate be reduced to $200/hour because she had less than one year of experience when she drafted the fees motion. But the Fitzpatrick Matrix places the 2021 D.C. rate for attorneys with less than one year of experience at $426/hour — before adjusting for California costs and inflation. (Serpik Decl., Exh. 8.) Ms. Aleagha's rate of $450/hour in 2023 is entirely consistent with this benchmark after appropriate adjustments. Plaintiff's proposed rate of $200/hour is below what was considered reasonable in D.C. in 2021 — let alone in Southern California in 2023.

V.
PLAINTIFF'S PROPOSED FEE OF $21,000 IS ARBITRARY AND PUNITIVE

Plaintiff's proposed reduction to $21,000 would slash the fee award by nearly 70%. This proposed amount bears no relationship to the work actually performed, the results obtained, or the prevailing market rates. Plaintiff arrives at $21,000 by simultaneously: (1) cutting the hours roughly in half without any specific identification of entries that are actually duplicative; (2) reducing the rates by 50% without any market evidence; and (3) compounding these arbitrary reductions.

Such a drastic reduction would itself be punitive — to the prevailing defendants. The Legislature mandated fee recovery under section 425.16(c) precisely to ensure that defendants who are targeted by meritless SLAPP suits can vindicate their rights without bearing the financial burden of doing so. Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1131. The fee-shifting provision "would be rendered meaningless" if courts were to slash fees to a fraction of their actual amount based on nothing more than opposing counsel's say-so. Id.

Attorney Defendants' total request of $66,060 is modest compared to fees routinely awarded in Anti-SLAPP cases. See, e.g., Marshall v. Webster (2020) 54 Cal.App.5th 275, 277 ($79,000); Lunada Biomedical v. Nunez (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 459, 488 ($104,293.75 and $57,765.63); Six4Three, LLC v. Facebook, Inc. (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 635, 660 (fees for Anti-SLAPP appeal recoverable). And this case involved a six-cause-of-action complaint, a fully-briefed Anti-SLAPP motion with extensive opposition, a full appeal with multiple subsidiary motions, and a complete victory at every level.

VI.
RESPONSE TO PLAINTIFF'S INAPPLICABLE AUTHORITY

Plaintiff relies heavily on Robertson v. Rodriguez (1995) 36 Cal.App.4th 347 and Olsen v. Harbison (2005) 134 Cal.App.4th 278 for the proposition that Anti-SLAPP fees should not be a "windfall." But neither case holds that fees should be slashed to a fraction of their actual value. Robertson merely holds that the trial court need not award the exact amount requested — a point Attorney Defendants do not dispute. And Olsen addresses the potential for abuse of the Anti-SLAPP statute itself, not the fee provision. Neither case supports Plaintiff's proposed 70% reduction.

Plaintiff also cites Kearney v. Foley and Lardner (S.D. Cal. 2008) 553 F.Supp.2d 1178, a federal case applying federal standards, for the proposition that fees should be reduced if hours are "excessive, redundant, or otherwise unnecessary." Attorney Defendants agree with this general proposition — but Plaintiff has not identified any specific entries that are actually excessive, redundant, or unnecessary. Plaintiff's argument amounts to nothing more than: "the total seems high, so cut it in half." That is not how the lodestar analysis works.

VII.
CONCLUSION

Attorney Defendants prevailed on the Anti-SLAPP motion. The Court of Appeal affirmed. Fees are mandatory. The hours are well-documented and reasonable. The rates are supported by objective market evidence. Plaintiff's Opposition offers no credible basis to reduce the award.

Attorney Defendants respectfully request that the Court award the full $66,060 in attorneys' fees, comprised of $20,940 for pre-appeal matters and $45,120 for appellate matters, and enter judgment accordingly.
