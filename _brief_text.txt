MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES
INTRODUCTION
Plaintiff Irma Mondragon was injured at work. While Defendants HOLIDAY INN EXPRESS & SUITESand BRANDEN BECENTI (collectively, “Demurring Defendants”) do not doubt that Plaintiff susustained injuries or that the April 25, 2023 assault she suffered was undeniably traumatic, the law does not permit her employer's failures to be transmuted into liability against an innocent bystander.
Plaintiff was a County of Riverside Child Protective Services employee. Her employer — the County — assigned her to supervise, alone and without backup, a violent juvenile ward of the court with a documented history of 5150 holds and prior physical attacks on CPS staff. (SAC ¶¶ 18–22.) That child was in the County's exclusive legal custody. (SAC ¶ 27.) The County housed him at the Holiday Inn Express in Corona. (SAC ¶ 18.) On April 25, 2023, that child attacked Plaintiff in the hotel's gymnasium. (SAC ¶ 22.) Plaintiff's own factual narrative, which the Court must accept on demurrer, establishes that Plaintiff called police for assistance but the police refused to intervene. (SAC ¶ __.) Meanwhile, Holiday Inn personnel saw the assault on surveillance and immediately called Plaintiff's CPS supervisor to notify her of the attack. (SAC ¶ 28.) 
That is the entirety of Holiday Inn and Branden Becenti's alleged involvement. They did not employ Plaintiff. They did not employ, supervise, or have custody over the attacker. They did not assign Plaintiff to the dangerous task, order her to physically restrain the child, or withhold information about the child's violent history. All of those alleged wrongs were committed by the County and its supervisors. (SAC ¶¶ 18–49.) 
Holiday Inn's and Becenti's only alleged conduct was passive observation followed by an affirmative call for help to the County official responsible for the child and for Plaintiff. Plaintiff seeks to bootstrap that conduct into three separate torts: "Intentional Misconduct," Negligent Hiring/Training/Supervision, and Premises Liability. As discussed below, each fails as a matter of law. The defects are matters of law, not draftsmanship. The Demurrer should be sustained without leave to amend.
PLAINTIFF’S ALLEGATIONS
Plaintiff was employed by the County of Riverside as a Social Services Assistant in CPS for approximately nine years. (SAC ¶ 16.) On April 25, 2023, her County supervisor, Noelia Sanchez, informed Plaintiff and others that they would be providing care to a CPS minor housed at the Holiday Inn Express, 1550 Circle City Drive, Corona. (SAC ¶ 18.) Sanchez and other County officials knew the minor had a history of violent episodes, including two 5150 calls and prior physical violence, assault, and battery toward providers. (SAC ¶ 19.) The County did not share this history with Plaintiff. (Ibid.) Sanchez ordered Plaintiff to physically restrain the child if he tried to leave in violation of County policy and notwithstanding Plaintiff's lack of restraint training. (SAC ¶¶ 20, 24.) 
While Plaintiff was caring for the child in the hotel gymnasium, the child attacked her for approximately forty minutes. (SAC ¶ 22.) The SAC alleges that Holiday Inn's surveillance system captured the attack and that Becenti and/or other hotel personnel observed it. (SAC ¶¶ 23, 28.) The SAC's specific allegations regarding the alleged conduct of Holiday Inn and Becenti are limited to the following:
"After the attack, Defendant Holiday Inn's General Manager, Defendant Branden Becenti indicated that he and/or other hotel staff had witnessed the event via camera footage of the gymnasium, and — recognizing the great harm or threat of harm posed to Ms. Mondragon — had called supervisor Sherry Gaylord-Jansen around 12:00pm to notify her of the ongoing attack. Becenti and other hotel staff did not attempt to intervene or assist Ms. Mondragon during the attack, only making contact with the supervisor and offering words of support and a copy of surveillance video afterward."

(SAC ¶ 28, emphasis added.)
Plaintiff also alleges that during the assault she called the police, but the police dispatcher informed her they could not assist "because the child is a ward of the court." (SAC ¶ 27.) She alleges that her County supervisor, Sherry Gaylord-Jansen, took no action other than to extend the child's custody period. (SAC ¶ 29.) She alleges that her County colleagues did not come to relieve her until approximately 3:00 p.m. (SAC ¶ 30.) The entire chain of operative decisions including who to assign, what training to provide, what custody arrangements to make, how to respond to the in-progress attack, was the County's.
The balance of the SAC concerns events occurring back at the County workplace, in which Holiday Inn and Becenti played no role and are not alleged to have played any role: alleged failure to accommodate Plaintiff's PTSD (SAC ¶¶ 31–39); denial of promotion (SAC ¶¶ 40, 45); continued solo assignments involving violent juveniles at County facilities (SAC ¶¶ 37, 42, 46); public disclosure of Plaintiff's medical information by County supervisors Swancy and Patel (SAC ¶¶ 47–48); and continuing harassment, scrutiny, and isolation by County managers (SAC ¶¶ 36, 49).
Of the twelve causes of action pled, nine are asserted exclusively against the County and its individual employees. Only the First (Intentional Misconduct), Second (Negligent Hiring, Training, and Supervision), and Third (Premises Liability) causes of action are asserted against Holiday Inn or Becenti. Each is addressed below.
LEGAL STANDARD
A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the factual allegations of a complaint. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.) Although the court must assume the truth of all properly pleaded material facts, it does not assume the truth of "contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law." (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)
Where, as here, the pleading is a Second Amended Complaint, leave to amend should be denied if it is clear that no amendment can cure the defect. "[L]eave to amend should not be granted where . . . amendment would be futile." (Vaillette v. Fireman's Fund Ins. Co. (1993) 18 Cal.App.4th 680, 685.) The Plaintiff bears the burden of showing how a defective pleading can be amended. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.)
ARGUMENT
THE FIRST CAUSE OF ACTION (INTENTIONAL MISCONDUCT) FAILS BECAUSE IT IS NOT A COGNIZABLE TORT, AND CONSTRUED AS IIED IT IS DEFICIENT AS A MATTER OF LAW
"Intentional Misconduct" Is Not a Recognized California Tort.
California does not recognize "Intentional Misconduct" as an independent cause of action. The label is a conclusory characterization, not a tort. (Cf. Lopez v. Southern Cal. Rapid Transit Dist. (1985) 40 Cal.3d 780, 795 [a tort must be identified by its essential elements].) On that ground alone, the First Cause of Action fails. Plaintiff must specify the precise tort she is asserting. (Careau & Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1371, 1390.)
The SAC Fails to Plead Extreme and Outrageous Conduct by Holiday Inn or Becenti.
Because the First Cause of Action references "intentional and malicious conduct," "harm and abuse," and emotional distress damages (SAC ¶¶ 61–67), it appears to attempt to plead Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress ("IIED"). Construed as such, it still fails.
IIED requires: "(1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff's suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant's outrageous conduct." (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1050.) The conduct must be "so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community." (Ibid.; Davidson v. City of Westminster (1982) 32 Cal.3d 197, 209.) "Liability for intentional infliction of emotional distress does not extend to mere insults, indignities, threats, annoyances, petty oppressions, or other trivialities." (Hughes, supra, 46 Cal.4th at p. 1051.) Whether conduct is sufficiently extreme is a question that may be  decided on demurrer. (Trerice v. Blue Cross of California (1989) 209 Cal.App.3d 878, 883.)
The SAC's IIED-style allegations against Holiday Inn and Becenti reduce to one factual proposition: hotel staff observed the attack on surveillance and did not physically intervene, though they did call Plaintiff's County supervisor. (SAC ¶¶ 23, 28, 63.) That is not extreme and outrageous conduct as a matter of law.
First, mere non-action, even with knowledge, is not "outrageous" where the defendant has no legal duty to act. (See Davidson, supra, 32 Cal.3d at pp. 209–210 [police observation of attack without intervention not actionable where no special relationship existed].) As demonstrated in Section IV.C, infra, California law does not impose a duty on a hotel operator to physically intervene in an ongoing assault. Failure to do what one has no duty to do is not extreme conduct.
Second, the SAC's own allegations describe affirmative effort by Holiday Inn to assist Plaintiff: Becenti called Gaylord-Jansen, Plaintiff's CPS supervisor and the official with authority over both Plaintiff and the child,
 to report the ongoing attack. (SAC ¶ 28.) That is a reasonable response, not outrageous indifference. If the County's response thereafter was inadequate (and the SAC alleges it was — see SAC ¶ 29), that failure is properly attributed to the County, not to Holiday Inn. A hotel manager cannot be required to assume the police powers, custodial powers, and supervisory authority that the SAC concedes belonged exclusively to the County.
Third, the SAC's bare incantation that Holiday Inn's omission was "intentional and malicious" (SAC ¶ 63) is precisely the kind of conclusory allegation that does not withstand demurrer. (Blank v. Kirwan, supra, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318.) Plaintiff cannot transform her own employer's negligence into the hotel's intentional tort merely by labeling the hotel's inaction as "intentional."
The SAC Fails to Plead Intent to Cause Severe Emotional Distress.
IIED requires conduct "directed at the plaintiff, or occurring in the presence of a plaintiff of whom the defendant is aware." (Christensen v. Superior Court (1991) 54 Cal.3d 868, 903.) The defendant must intend, or recklessly disregard the probability of causing, severe emotional distress. (Ibid.) The SAC alleges that Holiday Inn personnel watched and did nothing. Even on its face, this is a charge of indifference, not of intent to cause distress, much less of conduct directed at Plaintiff for the purpose of injuring her. The hotel had no animus toward Plaintiff and no incentive to harm her; she was a guest using its facilities in the course of her employer's business.
THE SECOND CAUSE OF ACTION (NEGLIGENT HIRING, TRAINING, AND SUPERVISION) FAILS BECAUSE THE SAC IDENTIFIES NO UNFIT EMPLOYEE WHO COMMITTED A TORTIOUS ACT.
The Cause of Action Requires Identification of an Employee Whose Tortious Conduct Caused Harm.
Negligent hiring, training, and supervision is a derivative tort. To state such a claim, a plaintiff must allege: (a) that the employer hired a particular employee; (b)	that the employee was unfit, incompetent, or had a particular propensity for the conduct that ultimately caused injury; (c) that the employer knew or should have known of the unfitness; (d) that the employee committed a wrongful act causing the plaintiff's injury; and (e) that the employer's negligence in hiring, training, or supervising the employee was a substantial factor in causing the harm. (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 875;) "[A]n employer's duty to provide a safe workplace turns on its own actions; it is not a strict liability or vicarious liability theory but rather requires the plaintiff to identify specific acts or omissions that breach a duty of care." (Phillips, supra, at p. 1141.)
Here, the Second Cause of Action does not name a single Holiday Inn employee, does not identify any employee's purported propensity, and does not allege any specific tortious act by any Holiday Inn employee that caused Plaintiff's injury. Instead, paragraph 69 of the SAC parrots the same generic allegations made in the Third (Premises Liability) cause of action; i.e.,  that Holiday Inn "failed to adequately train, supervise and adopt adequate protocols," "failed to take reasonable measures to protect the Plaintiff," and "failed to provide the protection of immediate intervention." (SAC ¶ 69.) That is a duplicative premises-based theory, not a negligent-hiring claim. (See Federico v. Superior Court (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 1207, 1213–1214 [negligent supervision claim fails where it is "merely a restatement" of premises liability theory].)
Even charitably read, the SAC alleges only that Holiday Inn's staff failed to intervene in an assault by a non-employee third party (a County ward). There is no Holiday Inn employee who committed a wrongful act against Plaintiff. The cause of action thus collapses for want of its essential predicate. (C.A. v. William S. Hart, supra, 53 Cal.4th at p. 875.)
The Cause of Action Cannot Lie Against Becenti as an Individual.
Negligent hiring, training, and supervision lies only against an employer. (See Mendoza v. City of Los Angeles (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 1333, 1339–1340.) Becenti is alleged to be Holiday Inn's General Manager, i.e., an employee. (SAC ¶ 5.) An employee does not hire, train, or supervise himself. As to Becenti, the Second Cause of Action is facially defective.

THE THIRD CAUSE OF ACTION (PREMISES LIABILITY) FAILS BECAUSE HOLIDAY INN HAD NO DUTY TO PHYSICALLY INTERVENE IN AN ASSAULT COMMITTED BY A COUNTY WARD.
A Landowner's Duty Regarding Third-Party Criminal Acts Is Limited and Calibrated by Foreseeability.
California landowners owe business invitees a duty of ordinary care. (Rowland v. Christian (1968) 69 Cal.2d 108, 119.) But duty to prevent third-party criminal conduct is not coextensive with general due care; rather, "[t]he scope of the duty is determined in part by balancing the foreseeability of the harm against the burden of the duty to be imposed." (Ann M. v. Pacific Plaza Shopping Center (1993) 6 Cal.4th 666, 678; see also Castaneda v. Olsher (2007) 41 Cal.4th 1205, 1213.)
The California Supreme Court has articulated a sliding scale: the more burdensome the proposed duty, the more highly foreseeable the harm must be before that duty arises. (Castaneda, supra, 41 Cal.4th at p. 1214; Delgado v. Trax Bar & Grill (2005) 36 Cal.4th 224, 243.) "[D]uties requiring no more than minimal attention or expense on the landowner's part may be imposed in the absence of heightened foreseeability . . . However, duties to take more burdensome and expensive precautions . . . will generally require some form of prior similar incidents or other indications of a reasonably foreseeable risk of [the harm]." (Castaneda, supra, at p. 1214.)
Where the proposed measure is hiring security guards (Ann M., supra), fencing common areas, or patrolling for gang activity, the California Supreme Court has consistently required "prior similar incidents" on the property or "other indications of a reasonably foreseeable risk." (Castaneda, supra, 41 Cal.4th at p. 1214; Ann M., supra, 6 Cal.4th at p. 679.) The duty Plaintiff seeks to impose here, that hotel staff physically intervene to halt an ongoing assault, is far more burdensome and dangerous than any duty previously recognized by California law. It would require untrained hotel personnel to physically engage with violent juveniles and strangers, exposing themselves and other guests to risk. It would impose quasi-law-enforcement obligations on private businesses. No California court has ever imposed such a duty.
The SAC does not allege a single prior incident of similar violence at this Holiday Inn property. It does not allege any history of CPS-related violence at the hotel. The most it alleges is that "the police had been called due to an incident with the child the previous night" at the same location (SAC ¶ 19) and that allegation does not even claim hotel staff knew of that police call. Under Ann M., Sharon P., and Castaneda, that is insufficient as a matter of law. "Absent any prior similar incidents or other indications of a reasonably foreseeable risk of violent criminal assaults in [the] location," no duty of heightened protection arises. (Ann M., supra, 6 Cal.4th at p. 679.)
Beyond the foreseeability analysis, controlling authority holds that there is no general duty to rescue or to physically intervene to protect another from third-party harm. (Williams v. State of California (1983) 34 Cal.3d 18, 23 ["[a]s a rule, one has no duty to come to the aid of another"]; see also Adams v. City of Fremont (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 243, 268–272 [no duty to intervene to prevent suicide]; Rest.2d Torts, § 314 ["The fact that the actor realizes or should realize that action on his part is necessary for another's aid or protection does not of itself impose upon him a duty to take such action."].)
Where a duty to act exists at all in the third-party context, it is generally the duty to take reasonable, non-burdensome measures such as calling the police or warning. (Delgado v. Trax Bar & Grill (2005) 36 Cal.4th 224, 247 ["[t]he proprietor of an establishment may have a duty to take reasonable steps, such as warning patrons or calling the authorities . . . ."].) The SAC affirmatively alleges that Holiday Inn discharged this minimal duty: Becenti called the County official with authority over both Plaintiff and the assailant — Gaylord-Jansen. (SAC ¶ 28.) Indeed, calling Plaintiff's own supervisor at her own employer was arguably more responsive than calling generic police, given that (a) the assailant was a court ward in County custody; (b) the police, when called, refused to respond because of that custody status (SAC ¶ 27); and (c) only the County had legal authority to remove the child from the hotel or impose custody-related controls.
Becenti Is Not a Proper Premises Liability Defendant.
Premises liability is a duty arising from the right to possess and control land. (Brooks v. Eugene Burger Management Corp. (1989) 215 Cal.App.3d 1611, 1619; CACI 1003.) A defendant who neither owns nor controls the property cannot be sued in premises liability. (Ibid.) The SAC alleges that Becenti was the General Manager of Holiday Inn — that is, Holiday Inn's employee. (SAC ¶ 5.) It does not allege he owned, leased, occupied, or had any independent possessory interest in the premises. As to Becenti individually, the Third Cause of Action is facially defective.
LEAVE TO AMEND SHOULD BE DENIED.


California courts routinely deny leave to amend a second amended complaint where the defects are matters of law. (See, e.g., Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 994 [denial of leave proper where amendments would be futile]; Vaillette, supra, 18 Cal.App.4th at p. 685.) Plaintiff cannot allege facts that did not occur: Holiday Inn employees were not her employer; the assailant was not their guest, ward, or employee; no prior similar incidents occurred on the premises; and the only conduct in which the hotel's personnel engaged (calling Plaintiff's supervisor) was the appropriate response. The Court should sustain the demurrer without leave to amend.
CONCLUSION
For the reasons set forth above, Demurring Defendants Holiday Inn Express & Suites and Branden Becenti respectfully request that the Court sustain their Demurrer to the First, Second, and Third Causes of Action of the Second Amended Complaint without leave to amend, and that the Court enter judgment of dismissal in favor of Demurring Defendants accordingly.
