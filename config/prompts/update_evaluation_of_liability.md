# Update Evaluation of Liability — AI Assistant for Word Prompt

## Usage
1. Open the updated report in Word (the copy where you've added/removed sections)
2. Highlight the old EVALUATION OF LIABILITY section
3. Set the AI Assistant to see the entire document as context
4. Paste the prompt below

---

## Prompt

**Objective:** Update the "EVALUATION OF LIABILITY" section for an updated litigation report.

**Critical Instruction — How to Use the Prior Evaluation:**
The highlighted text is the EVALUATION OF LIABILITY from the prior report. It serves two purposes, and you MUST treat them differently:

1. **FACTS to preserve:** The prior evaluation references evidence, documents, witness statements, depositions, and factual findings that may no longer appear elsewhere in this updated report (because they were already reported). These underlying facts remain true and relevant. You must carry them forward into your updated analysis.

2. **CONCLUSIONS to re-evaluate from scratch:** The prior evaluation's legal assessments, likelihood predictions, strength/weakness characterizations, and overall liability conclusions must NOT be carried forward automatically. You must independently re-evaluate every conclusion in light of ALL evidence — both the facts carried from the prior evaluation AND the new information in the updated report.

**Think of it this way:** Extract the facts from the old evaluation, combine them with the new facts from the updated report, then perform a completely fresh legal analysis on the combined fact base.

**How to Handle New vs. Previously Reported Information:**

- The updated report contains new information that was not available when the prior evaluation was written. This new information should receive **slightly more analytical focus** — explain its significance, how it affects each element, and whether it strengthens or weakens the prior assessment.

- Where new evidence **confirms** the prior analysis, use transitional language such as: "As previously reported, [factual point]. The recent [discovery/deposition/investigation] further supports this conclusion, as..."

- Where new evidence **changes** the prior analysis, be direct: "Our prior report noted [prior assessment]. However, [new evidence] now indicates that..." or "In light of [new development], our assessment of this element has changed."

- Where new evidence **introduces entirely new issues** not addressed in the prior evaluation (e.g., a new cause of action, a new defense, a newly discovered comparative fault argument), give these their own full sub-heading treatment with the same depth as other elements.

- Where previously reported facts remain **unchanged and unaffected by new evidence**, you may summarize them more concisely than in the original (e.g., "As established in our prior report, Defendant owned and operated the property. This element remains undisputed.") rather than repeating the full analysis.

**The final output must be a complete, self-contained EVALUATION OF LIABILITY** — a reader who never saw the prior report should still be able to understand the full liability picture. Do not write an addendum or supplement; write a complete section.

**Task:** Replace the highlighted text with an updated EVALUATION OF LIABILITY section, analyzing the provided case information according to the instructions below.

**Style, Tone, and Structure Requirements:**

1. **Heading:** Start the section with the exact heading "EVALUATION OF LIABILITY" in all caps.
2. **Introduction:** Briefly state the causes of action alleged by the Plaintiff(s) against the Defendant(s).
3. **Structure by Cause of Action:**
   - Organize the analysis by each distinct cause of action (e.g., Breach of Contract, Negligence, Dangerous Condition of Public Property, Strict Product Liability, Negligent Hiring/Training/Supervision, Premises Liability). Use clear sub-headings for each (e.g., "A. Breach of Contract", "B. Negligence").
   - For each cause of action, **first state the required legal elements** needed for the Plaintiff to prevail. Include legal citations (CACI numbers, Government Code sections, landmark cases) where applicable under California law.
   - **Analyze each relevant element systematically** with sub-headings where appropriate (e.g., "Duty", "Breach", "Causation", "Damages", "Ownership", "Dangerous Condition", "Notice", "Harm", "Substantial Factor").
   - **Apply specific facts:** For each element, discuss how the specific facts apply. Reference specific documents, evidence, deposition testimony, and factual details.
   - **Present both sides:** Articulate the Plaintiff's likely arguments and the Defendant's likely defenses. Use predictive phrasing: "Plaintiff will argue...", "We will argue...", "Claimant may contend...".
   - **Assess likelihood/viability:** Include assessments using phrases like: "Plaintiffs will likely be able to establish this element," "we believe the City has a strong defense," "viable pathway to establish," "Plaintiffs will have a difficult time establishing."
   - **Acknowledge unknowns:** If information is missing or further investigation is needed, state that clearly: "Further discovery is necessary...", "We will provide you with a further update..."
4. **Concluding Summary:** End with a "Summary" sub-heading providing the overall liability assessment — key strengths, weaknesses, and how the updated information has affected the analysis.
5. **Tone:** Formal, objective, analytical, professional. Perspective of defense counsel evaluating potential liability for the insurance carrier.
6. **Language:** Use precise legal terminology (respondeat superior, vicarious liability, dangerous condition, comparative fault, contributory negligence, substantial factor, CACI, Gov. Code).
7. **Formatting:** Use formatting consistent with the rest of the report (bolded sub-headings, indentation, flowing prose paragraphs — not bullet points in the body).

**Scope:**
- Focus exclusively on evaluating liability. Do not address damages valuation, settlement recommendations, or procedural history unless directly relevant to a liability argument.
- Do not fabricate information. Base the analysis solely on facts present in the document and the prior evaluation.
- The output must flow logically and read as a cohesive analysis section.

**Professional Standards:**
- Formal legal writing style appropriate for client reports
- Cite specific facts, documents, and legal authorities
- Maintain objectivity while advocating for the client's position
- Provide actionable insights for case strategy
- Consider California law and comparative fault principles throughout
